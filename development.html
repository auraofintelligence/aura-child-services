10 / CARE SERVICES, FAMILIES AND SEPARATE SCHOOL PILOTS

Begin where people can follow through.

A useful pilot needs a willing host, time, support and something concrete to try. Young people help shape it, and what happens changes the next step.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROPOSAL

Starting through care services and adoptive families

A first pilot begins with a willing service, appropriate cultural and legal partners, young people and supportive adults. They identify a real opportunity for improvement: continuity of a chosen project, understanding a care-related decision, access to a mentor or a more useful transition into community learning.

Making the invitation concrete

The host explains the activities, people involved, equipment, location, recording choices and support. Young people with lived experience of care or adoption help shape the language and practical arrangements. The invitation describes something understandable to try and a way to decline without losing other support.

Adults practise the proposed activity using example information. They demonstrate a correction, check a legal source, restore a saved project and end a shared-station session. These checks establish whether the equipment and support are dependable enough for the intended use. The first learner then begins with a chosen interest and an achievable result.

Resourcing the human work

The plan identifies staff and mentor time, cultural participation, legal review, accessibility, equipment, transport, software, storage, maintenance and evaluation. A suitable refurbished computer may meet part of the need; a trained adult and reliable follow-through remain essential. Funding comes from documented service, grant, enterprise or other lawful arrangements. C-Hour recognition and community incentives are described alongside those costs, without replacing the budget.

The programme connects its learning activities with existing care and transition plans only to the agreed extent. A child's private reflection does not become a routine report for every stakeholder. The host records who responds to questions, how complaints are handled and what happens if the activity ends or a family moves.

Brief school pilots

A small number of school pilots would explore selected activities such as creative projects, source checking, civics or understanding AI through a practical task. Teachers choose the curriculum connection and review learning. Participation is offered without identifying pupils by care or adoption history, and school material remains separate from private reflections. The Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools provides relevant guidance, alongside the school system's actual approval, privacy, age and consent requirements. [S14]

Care-service and school experiences may then inform one another through approved teaching material and findings. The child chooses suitable work to carry between settings. The pilot grows only where the evidence, people and resources support a useful next step.

Judging whether lives are improving

The proposal offers reasons to investigate benefits, rather than evidence that the whole approach already produces them. Evaluation follows what matters in the young person's life and whether the experience made a useful difference. Their own account remains central alongside observed tasks and the experience of adults and hosts.

Benefit sought

Evidence to examine

A stronger voice

The learner understands a decision, expresses a view and sees how the responsible person responds.

Continuity and self understanding

Chosen work is found again, a summary is corrected and a later encounter reflects what the learner explained.

Growing knowledge and judgement

The learner follows a source, explains a result and applies something learned in a changed situation.

Real opportunities

A mentor, placement, equipment invitation or suitable event actually becomes available and useful.

A stronger social fabric

Relationships continue, newcomers are welcomed and contributors also know where to seek support.

A supported transition into adulthood

Learning connects with actual housing, income, relationships, care and the next chosen opportunity.

Experience that changes the proposal

A young person may find the conversation intrusive, the tool frustrating or the promised opportunity unavailable. A host may discover that the activity needs more staff time than planned. Those findings change the programme. The review includes people who leave and tasks that fail, together with any incident or unintended burden.

The host checks whether technology adds useful contact or displaces it, whether legal explanations match checked sources and whether information stays within its stated audience. A serious problem prompts appropriate action and a review before the affected function is used again. Research involving children follows the relevant ethical review and consent arrangements; an educational setting does not settle that assessment by itself. [S15]

An improvement with somewhere to go

The first successful result may be modest and personally important. A child completes a game, finds a welcoming mentor and understands a question about their rights. That experience gives the next activity a better starting point. Across many such encounters, care services and communities discover which connections are worth sustaining.

The intended direction remains substantial: children growing into adults who understand themselves, know how to investigate the rules around them and participate in the benefits and responsibilities of increasingly powerful intelligence. Their next step is chosen through a real relationship, with something useful available to do.
